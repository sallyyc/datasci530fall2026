Introduction¶

For this project, I analyzed the FDA recall announcements collected in Project 1 to understand how the listings differ across product groups, recall reasons, years, and companies. I also explored whether missing termination information is related to recall year, as suggested in my Project 1 feedback. Each observation represents one listing, which may cover multiple products or brands.

Key findings¶

The following findings summarize the analysis below.

  • Food listings make up most of the dataset: 777 of the 953 listings include Food & Beverages, while 178 include Drugs; two listings include both.
  • The selected recall-reason themes differ by product group: 40.4% of food listings mention undeclared ingredients or allergens. Among drug listings, 15.7% mention that theme and 14.6% mention methanol; a listing can match multiple themes.
  • Company appearances are widely distributed, with only six listings for each of the two most frequently recorded company names. These are listing counts rather than measures of company safety.
  • Recent listings more often lack termination information: the percentage missing ranges from 75.0% to 85.2% in 2024–2026, compared with 9.9% in 2023. This supports an association with year without establishing that blank entries represent ongoing recalls.

Data source and collection¶

I collected the data on October 20, 2026, from the FDA Recalls, Market Withdrawals, and Safety Alerts table. In Project 1, I used Selenium to select Food & Beverages and Drugs, display 100 entries per page, and move through the results automatically. The collection included seven listing fields, with two repeated records removed because they appeared in both searches.

I did not apply a date filter, visit the separate archive, or collect information from individual announcement pages. As a result, the dataset describes the listings available in the main table at collection time rather than a complete historical record of FDA recalls. The dates range from October 19, 2017, to October 17, 2026.

This uneven coverage makes it difficult to interpret differences across years as changes in recall activity. The figure is more useful for showing the composition of this dataset and the years that contribute most to the remaining analysis.

Repeated listings describe repeated appearances in this dataset, not the number of individual products recalled or a company's safety record.

Response to Project 1 feedback: missing termination information¶

My instructor suggested examining whether blank Terminated Recall fields are partly explained by recall year, since more recent recalls may still be ongoing. I compared the percentage missing within each year and then separated the results by product group. The annual table includes sample sizes because estimates based on a few older listings can vary substantially.

A blank field means that termination information was not recorded in the collected table. It does not confirm that a recall is ongoing, so this analysis evaluates an association with year rather than the reason information is missing.

Missing termination information is more common among recent listings: 79.7% in 2024, 75.0% in 2025, and 85.2% in 2026, compared with 9.9% in 2023 and 11.8% in 2022. This is consistent with the instructor's expectation, although the relationship is not a steady increase across every year. Older years also contain fewer observations, and missingness differs between the product groups.

Recall year therefore helps characterize which records lack termination information, but it does not fully explain the blanks. The data do not include termination dates, follow-up histories, or a confirmed status for blank entries, so I cannot determine whether recency, reporting practices, or other factors account for the pattern.

Discussion and limitations¶

This analysis shows that the collected announcements cover several kinds of products and safety concerns, with food-related listings making up most of the dataset. The recall-reason descriptions and additional FDA labels provide complementary information: the descriptions identify specific concerns, while the labels help place them in a broader context. Company listings are distributed across many recorded names, which limits how much a short ranking alone can tell us.

The missingness analysis also shows why blank values should be examined rather than treated as a single recall status. Recent listings are more likely to lack termination information, but missingness is not determined by year alone.

These findings describe the announcements available in the selected main-table searches. They do not measure recall rates, the number of affected products, the severity of an event, or consumer exposure. The absence of archived records limits historical comparisons, and the incomplete 2026 period limits annual comparisons further. Overlapping category labels, variations in company names, and free-text descriptions also affect how the results can be grouped. Within those limits, the report identifies useful patterns while preserving the distinction between what is recorded and what can be concluded.
