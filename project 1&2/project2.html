Project 2: Patterns in FDA Food and Drug Recall Announcements¶

Sally Choi

Project Overview¶

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) maintains the Recalls, Market Withdrawals, and Safety Alerts website to inform the public about products that may pose health or safety risks. Its listings describe affected products, company and brand names, recall reasons, and whether a recall is marked as terminated. The announcements cover different concerns, including undeclared ingredients, possible contamination, and manufacturing problems.

For Project 1, I collected seven fields from the FDA results table on October 20, 2026, restricting the searches to Food & Beverages and Drugs. For this project, I imported that dataset into a separate notebook to examine the distribution of listings over time, compare recall reasons across the two groups, and describe the products and companies represented. I also looked more closely at specific allergens mentioned in food listings and addressed the Project 1 feedback about whether missing termination information varies by recall year. Each observation represents one listing, which may describe more than one product or brand.

Key findings¶

Food & Beverages accounts for most of the dataset, with 777 of the 953 listings, compared with 178 for Drugs; two listings belong to both groups. The selected recall-reason themes differ across the groups: 40.4% of food listings mention undeclared ingredients or allergens, while 15.7% of drug listings mention that theme and 14.6% mention methanol. Within the 314 food listings matching the undeclared-ingredient or allergen theme, milk is the most frequently mentioned of the six ingredients examined, appearing in 92 descriptions. Company appearances are spread across 808 recorded names, with only six listings each for the two most frequently recorded names. Finally, missing termination information is more common in recent years, ranging from 75.0% to 85.2% in 2024–2026, compared with 9.9% in 2023. This pattern is consistent with a relationship between year and missing information, although a blank field does not confirm that a recall is ongoing.

Data source and collection¶

I collected the data from the FDA Recalls, Market Withdrawals, and Safety Alerts table on October 20, 2026. The Project 1 algorithm used Selenium to select Food & Beverages and Drugs, display 100 entries per page, and click Next until the results were collected. It recorded Date, Brand Name(s), Product Description, Product Type, Recall Reason Description, Company Name, and Terminated Recall. Two listings appeared in both searches, so I removed the repeated copies while preserving their original category labels.

I did not apply a date filter, visit the separate archive, or collect information from individual announcement pages. The dates in the saved data range from October 19, 2017, to October 17, 2026, but that range does not establish complete coverage of every year. The report describes the main-table listings available when I collected them rather than all FDA recalls during that period.

Data preparation and overview¶

I treated empty or whitespace-only cells as missing and converted Date to a date format so that I could extract the listing year. I also created an indicator for missing termination information and kept separate food and drug membership indicators. These indicators preserve the two listings that belong to both groups, so group counts add to 955 even though the combined dataset has 953 observations. Percentages within each product group use that group's own listing count, while percentages described as shares of the full dataset use 953 as the denominator.

The first two tables summarize completeness and group membership. I also checked for duplicate rows across the seven collected fields and for listings outside both selected product groups.

Reasons for recalls¶

Recall Reason Description is a free-text field, so the same concern can be written in different ways. I selected six themes to compare several types of concerns recorded in the recall descriptions: Listeria, Salmonella, undeclared ingredients or allergens, sterility, lead, and methanol. These themes cover pathogen-related concerns, ingredient disclosure, sterility, and chemical contamination, allowing a focused comparison between food and drug listings. They are not an exhaustive classification or necessarily the six most common recall reasons. For example, the sterility search matches words containing “steril,” while the lead search requires a whole-word match.

For each theme, I calculated the percentage of listings in each product group whose description matched the search. A description can match more than one theme, so the percentages are not intended to add to 100%. Listings without any selected terms remain in the denominator. The themes provide a focused comparison instead of a complete classification.

Recall-reason findings¶

Undeclared ingredients or allergens appear in 40.4% of food listings, making this the largest of the six selected themes within that group. Salmonella appears in 18.7%, followed by Listeria at approximately 17.6%, which shows that both ingredient disclosure and pathogen-related concerns are prominent in the collected food descriptions. The combined “undeclared ingredients or allergens” theme is broader than confirmed allergen recalls because the search also includes other undeclared ingredients.

For Drugs, 15.7% of listings mention undeclared ingredients or allergens and 14.6% mention methanol. Methanol appears much more frequently within drug listings than food listings, while the pathogen themes are more prominent among food listings. These differences describe the wording of the recorded reasons and help identify concerns worth examining more closely; they do not compare the severity of recalls or represent every possible reason in either group.

Products and additional FDA labels¶

The Product Description field contains detailed free text rather than a consistent product classification. Instead of assigning subjective product categories, I used the additional labels already recorded in Product Type. The table includes product labels and safety labels, so it should not be read as a ranking of mutually exclusive product categories. Repeated labels within one listing are counted once, while a listing can contribute to several labels.

I split the recorded category text at commas, removed repeated labels within the same listing, and excluded the two main search labels before ranking the remaining terms. Because this method follows the source's comma-separated text, a compound label such as “Spices, Flavors & Salts” is displayed as separate terms. I refer to the results as additional labels rather than treating every extracted term as a distinct product category.

Specific allergens mentioned in food listings¶

To examine the largest selected food-reason theme in more detail, I restricted this analysis to food listings whose descriptions mention “undeclared” or “allergen.” I then searched those descriptions for six specific ingredients: milk, wheat, soy, egg, peanut, and sesame. The searches use whole words and include common plural forms, with soybean also included under soy.

The denominator is the 314 food listings matching the broader undeclared-ingredient or allergen search, rather than all food listings. These six ingredients are a selected set, so the chart does not include every possible allergen. A description can mention several ingredients, and a match does not establish whether that ingredient was undeclared rather than appearing elsewhere in the explanation.

I counted appearances by Company Name after trimming spaces at the beginning and end of each value. I retained the source's spelling and did not combine subsidiaries, related businesses, or similar names, so the results describe recorded company names rather than independently verified corporate entities. A company can appear in several listings because of separate announcements, affected products, or updates, which the listing-level dataset cannot fully distinguish. These counts therefore describe repeated appearances in the collected table rather than independent safety incidents.

Missing termination information¶

A suggestion from project 1 was made to examine whether blank Terminated Recall fields are partly explained by recall year, since more recent recalls may still be ongoing. I calculated the number and percentage of missing values within each year, then compared the percentages separately for Food & Beverages and Drugs. The annual table includes both overall and group-specific observation counts so that large percentage differences can be considered alongside the number of listings supporting them.

A blank field means that termination information was not recorded in the collected table; it does not confirm that a recall is ongoing. This analysis therefore examines whether missingness is associated with year, while leaving the reason for individual blank entries unresolved. A gap in a group's line indicates a year with no listings for that group, rather than a missing rate of zero.

Missing termination information is much more common in recent years: 79.7% of listings in 2024, 75.0% in 2025, and 85.2% in 2026 have blank fields, compared with 9.9% in 2023 and 11.8% in 2022. This is consistent with the expectation that recent recalls may be less likely to have reached a recorded termination, although the relationship is not a steady increase across every year. Missingness also differs across product groups; all drug listings dated 2025 and 2026 lack termination information in this dataset.

Older-year percentages needs more care when examining because the dataset has only two listings in 2017 and three in 2018. So, a single blank field can substantially change the percentage when the denominator is that small. The larger recent-year totals make the high missing percentages more broadly representative of the collected recent listings, but they still do not explain why a field is blank.

Recall year helps characterize which records lack termination information, but the data do not include termination dates, follow-up histories, or a confirmed status for blank entries. I therefore cannot separate the effects of time since listing, product-group differences, and reporting practices. The main response to the feedback is that missingness is patterned by year and product group, so interpreting every blank as an ongoing recall would hide meaningful differences in the available information.

Discussion and limitations¶

The collected announcements reveal several related patterns: food listings make up most of the dataset, selected recall-reason themes differ between food and drugs, and repeated company appearances are distributed across many recorded names. The additional FDA labels broaden the picture by identifying overlapping safety concerns and product areas, while the ingredient analysis shows which specific allergens are mentioned within the selected food descriptions. Together, these summaries explain both the composition of the data and the concerns recorded in the announcements.

The missingness analysis adds an important qualification to the results. Termination information is less complete among recent listings, and the pattern also differs between food and drugs, so blanks should remain a separate information category rather than being assigned a confirmed status. This addresses the Project 1 feedback while preserving the limits of what the collected table can establish.

The findings describe listings available in the two selected main-table searches at one point in time. They do not measure recall rates, affected units, severity, or consumer exposure, and the absence of archived records limits historical comparisons. The partial 2026 period, overlapping product labels, variations in company names, and text-based reason searches also affect interpretation. Within these limits, the analysis provides a clearer description of the announcements without treating listing counts or missing fields as evidence of risk or recall status.
